# EIGHTFOLD / FINDING 10
## Oregon spent 78% of what it got. Indiana spent 10%. Same settlement, same year of the same crisis. And the entire national settlement is smaller than what the federal government misplaces in four months.

**Status:** State figures **CONFIRMED [P]** from two primary state filings. The
comparison figures in §5 are **[S]** — quoted from GAO's product page, primary not
captured, and §5 says why.
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-31.
**Opened at Jay Puckett's instruction to pull more states, and his observation:**

> *"that amount of money is nothing in comparison to what is wasted or put to things
> not needed here or stolen etc"*

**He is right, and §5 puts a number on it rather than agreeing with it.**

## 2. Against Indiana [P], and the gap is the finding

| | **Oregon FY2024-25** | **Indiana FY2023** |
|---|---|---|
| Local money received | $30.46M | $49.74M |
| Local money spent | **$23.81M** | **$5.01M** |
| **Share spent** | **78.2%** | **10.1%** |
| Units reporting nothing spent | not reported this way | **489 of 606 (80.7%)** |
| Legal fees | **$43,930 (0.18%)** | in a pooled admin/legal line |
| Administrative | **$93,460 (0.4%, against a 5% cap)** | — |

**Stated limit before anyone else states it: these are different fiscal years.**
Oregon's is FY2024-25 and Indiana's is FY2023, one year further into the disbursement
cycle. **Some of the gap is maturity, and a fair comparison needs Indiana's FY2025
filing, which is action item 1.** But a 68-point spread is not a year of ramp-up, and
Indiana's own filing shows 489 of 606 units that had not even *designated* the money.

**[I] Finding 08 reported, from KFF, that roughly a third of national settlement money
was spent, a third set aside and a third untrackable. These two states show what that
average is hiding.** It is not that every state spends a third. It is that some states
spend nearly all of it and some spend almost none, and the national figure is the
midpoint of two different behaviors.

**Fifty-six percent of Oregon's local settlement spending went to supporting people in
treatment and recovery and connecting them to care.** That is the closest thing this
case file has found to money going where Finding 02's evidence points.

**And Oregon says the mechanism out loud.** On its $13.32M Recovery Community Centers
line, the report describes them as helping people build:

> "**recovery capital** such as **employment opportunities, housing, and community
> connections**"

**That is Finding 02's model, named, in a state government's budget document.** It is
funded at $13.32 million.

**Criminal justice absorbed $1.88M, 7.9%**, and syringe services got **$4,071**, which
is one four-thousandth of Oregon's local disbursement.

## 5. Puckett's comparison, with a number on it [S]

> *"that amount of money is nothing in comparison to what is wasted."*

**GAO-26-108694, published 2026-04-27:**

> "15 agencies reported a total estimate of about **$186 billion in improper
> payments** across 64 programs"

> "Since fiscal year 2003, cumulative improper payment estimates... have totaled about
> **$3 trillion**"

**$24 billion more than FY2024.** About **82%, roughly $153 billion, were
overpayments** — money that went out and should not have. **19 programs ran improper
payment rates of at least 10%; six exceeded 25%.** And inspectors general found that
**only half of the 24 agencies responsible for most improper payments fully complied**
with the Payment Integrity Information Act.

**Now the arithmetic he asked for:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Federal improper payments, **FY2025 alone** | **$186,000,000,000** |
| **Entire national opioid settlement, ~20 years** | **$58,580,000,000** |
| Ratio | **3.2 : 1** |
| Days of improper payments to equal the whole settlement | **≈ 115** |
| Cumulative improper payments since 2003 | **~$3,000,000,000,000** |
| Settlement as a share of that | **≈ 2.0%** |
| SAMHSA employment-titled grants FY2024-25 (Finding 09) | **$73,800,000** |
| Days of improper payments to equal it | **≈ 0.14 — about three and a half hours** |

**He is right, and it is worse than "nothing in comparison."** The federal government
mis-pays in **under four months** the entire sum a dozen corporations agreed to pay
over two decades for their role in the overdose crisis. **The twenty-four employment
programs in the country cost about three and a half hours of it.**

**Two honest limits, and they matter.**
- **"Improper" is not "stolen."** GAO is explicit that improper payments include
  documentation failures and eligibility-verification gaps, not only fraud. **A large
  share is money that went to the right person with the wrong paperwork. This finding
  does not call it theft and neither should any OWG output.**
- **These figures are [S].** `gao.gov` returns **HTTP 403** to scripted fetch and
  `paymentaccuracy.gov` is a single-page app serving the same 23KB shell for every
  path including `/api` and `/data`. **The quotes came through a rendering fetch of
  GAO's product page. The primary is not captured** and both need a browser.

**[I] The point survives both limits.** Whether the $186 billion is fraud or filing
errors, it is money the federal government cannot account for, at a scale that makes
the entire opioid settlement a rounding error — **and the settlement is the thing
everyone is arguing about, while the rounding error is not being argued about at all.**

## 6. What this finding does NOT claim

- **No allegation of fraud, theft or misconduct** against any agency, state or
  official. GAO's category is "improper," and §5 keeps it there.
- **Two states are not fifty.** Oregon and Indiana are not a sample.
- **The fiscal years differ** and §2 says so; the comparison is indicative, not clean.
- **Oregon's 78% is a disbursement rate, not an outcome.** Nothing here says the money
  worked, only that it moved.
- **Nothing here says improper-payment money could or should be redirected to
  addiction.** That is a political argument and this is an arithmetic one.
