# EIGHTFOLD / FINDING 03
## The national addiction statistics put everyone from 26 to 100 in one box. Inside that box, the worst-affected age group in America is hiding, and it is the one trying to buy a first house.

**Status:** CONFIRMED [P] from SAMHSA's own detailed tables.
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-31.
**Opened at Jay Puckett's observation: "26+ is an age range that obfuscates the
truth, it's huge."** He is right, and the consequence is larger than presentation.

**This finding corrects a statement Aegis made earlier the same day.**

Exhibits: `exhibits/nsduh/`.

---

## 1. What the published report shows

The *2024 National Survey on Drug Use and Health* annual national report is the
document every journalist, legislator and agency quotes. **Every substance measure
in it is broken out four ways and only four ways: 12 or older, 12-17, 18-25, and
26 or older.**

**Checked with a control, because a negative claimed without one is worthless
here.** The report *does* carry a finer adult split — "26 to 49" and "50 or older"
— **67 times**, so the string is present and the search works. Every one of those
67 is a **mental health** measure: major depressive episode, any mental illness,
generalized anxiety. Walking all 109 table and figure captions and classifying
them, the finer split reaches exactly **one** substance-side table, **A.70B**, and
that one is *"Received Substance Use Treatment **or Mental Health Treatment**"* —
it inherits the mental-health scheme because it is half a mental-health table.

**So: SAMHSA already publishes adult substance data at finer resolution, and
already prints finer adult brackets in this very report for a different topic. The
substance tables are the ones that do not get it.**

**"26 or Older" is a fifty-to-seventy-five-year bucket** holding roughly three
quarters of the adult population.

Read at that resolution, the story is: young adults 18-25 are the problem group at
**25.9%**, and everyone else is at **16.4%**.

## 3. The specific thing it hides

**The highest-prevalence age group in the United States is 21-25 at 28.8%, and the
second is 26-29 at 27.9%. Neither appears anywhere in the national report.**

**26-29 sits above the published 18-25 figure of 25.9%.** The single worst-affected
adult age band is on the far side of the line that the report draws, so the report
cannot show it, and the reader is left with "it's a young adult problem that people
grow out of after 25."

**People do not grow out of it after 25.** They grow out of it after **45**, and the
decline from there is gradual: 22.6 at 35-39, 19.1 at 40-44, 15.0 at 50-54, 8.0 at
65+. The published bucket compresses a two-decade plateau and a slow twenty-year
decline into one number that describes neither.

## 4. The correction Aegis owes

**Earlier on 2026-08-31, testing H-11, I reported that "18-25s carry the highest SUD
prevalence, 25.9% vs 16.4% for 26+."** That was read off the collapsed bucket. It is
wrong in a way that mattered to the hypothesis it was testing.

**The corrected version strengthens H-11's cross-sectional half rather than
weakening it.** The peak-prevalence bands are **21-25 (28.8) and 26-29 (27.9)** —
which is precisely the first-time-homebuyer window, and precisely the cohort whose
ownership transition collapsed (H-11 addendum: the under-35-to-35-44 conversion fell
from +31.1 points to +17.6). **The overlap is now tight rather than approximate.**

**It is still not causation, and the ecological fallacy in H-11 is unchanged.** Two
population-level curves overlapping is not evidence about individuals. What changed
is that the curves overlap at all, which at the published resolution they appeared
not to.

## 5. The trend the bucket also hides [P]

Table 5.3B carries 2023 and 2024 side by side. Split by substance:

| Age | Drug UD 23 -> 24 | Alcohol UD 23 -> 24 |
|---|---|---|
| 26-29 | 16.9 -> **18.1** | 16.5 -> 15.1 |
| 30-34 | 13.2 -> **13.9** | 16.1 -> 13.3 |
| 35-39 | 12.1 -> **12.7** | 13.0 -> 13.7 |
| 40-44 | 9.0 -> **9.7** | 13.4 -> 12.0 |
| 45-49 | 9.8 -> **10.2** | 11.5 -> 11.1 |
| 26-49 | 12.1 -> **12.8** | 14.1 -> 13.0 |

**Drug use disorder rose in every adult band from 26 to 49. Alcohol use disorder
fell in four of five.** The headline "SUD declined, 17.1 to 16.8" is an **alcohol**
result carrying a **drug** result in the opposite direction underneath it.

**[I] Stated with the caveat it needs:** SAMHSA flags none of these six individual
year-over-year moves as significant at .05. **Six same-direction moves across six
independent age bands is a pattern, not six coincidences, but this file does not
claim statistical significance SAMHSA declined to claim.** The national total for
drug use disorder also rose, 9.6 -> 9.8, and that one is on the front page of nothing.

## 6. And the same bucket hides who gets help [P]

Table 5.14B, substance use treatment received, 2024:

| Age | Received treatment |
|---|---|
| 26-49 | **4.7%** |
| 50 or older | 2.8% |
| **65 or older** | **1.6%** |
| 12-17 | 2.8% |

**Within "26 or Older," treatment access falls by a factor of three from the
youngest end to the oldest.** A 66-year-old with a substance use disorder — 8.0%
prevalence, a real population — receives treatment at **one third** the rate of a
40-year-old. Pooled into 26+, that disappears into 3.7%.

**SAMHSA's own note on this table warns of "added uncertainty because of the high
proportion of respondents in the 'substance unspecified' category," and that the
2024 questions changed.** Carry both caveats with any use of these numbers. The
2023 column is published as `--`, not comparable.

## 7. What this finding does and does not say

**It does not allege that anyone chose these brackets to conceal anything**, and
that question was tested rather than assumed.

**The test.** Pulled the **2019** annual report, five years earlier, and counted:
`exhibits/nsduh/2019_nsduh_annual_national_report.pdf`. "26 or Older" **202**
times, "26 to 49" **57**, "50 or Older" **63**, "26 to 34" **0**. **Every one of the
57 finer-bracket instances is a major depressive episode, any mental illness, or
serious mental illness passage** — the same topic-specific division as 2024.

**So it is a convention, stable across at least five years, not a recent decision
about the current numbers.** That is the honest reading and it points at inertia.

**And there is a defensible technical reason underneath it:** the substance-use
headline series runs back two decades on those brackets, and changing them breaks
the trend comparison everyone quotes.

**But that reason does not survive contact with the same document.** The mental
health series in the 2019 report is quoted back to **2005 and 2008 baselines** and
carries the finer adult brackets anyway. **Comparability was preserved on one topic
while being finer on it, and used as the reason to stay coarse on the other.**

**[I] What that supports is not concealment.** It supports something duller and
more durable: **on the mental health side someone decided the adult population was
heterogeneous enough to need splitting, and on the substance side nobody has
revisited that question in twenty years.** The detailed tables exist, are free, and
require nobody's permission. **The work of looking has simply never been done by
the people writing the document that gets read.**

**What it says is narrower and harder to dismiss: the resolution at which this
country reports its addiction problem is too coarse to see the worst-affected group
in it,** and the document that everyone actually reads is the coarse one.

**This is the same defect EIGHTFOLD documents everywhere else.** Finding 56 in
SEVENFOLD: no instrument exists to count 7-OH deaths. Finding 02: the discriminating
experiment on recovery has never been run. **Here the instrument exists, was funded,
was administered to tens of thousands of people, and the answer is sitting in a zip
file that the summary report does not print.**
