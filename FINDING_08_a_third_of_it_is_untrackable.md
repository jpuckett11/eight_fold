# EIGHTFOLD / FINDING 08
## $58.58 billion in settlement money. A third of the first tranche cannot be traced at all, because the jurisdictions that took it never filed a public report. And on the one dimension this case has been arguing about, the settlement money is allocated better than the federal research money.

## 1. The size of it [S]

**$58.58 billion.** OpioidSettlementTracker.com, as of **2026-04-14**, verbatim:

> "Pending finalization of Albertsons' **$774 million** opioid settlement, the Global
> Settlement Total will be **$58.58 billion**."

Paid by, per the Plaintiffs' Executive Committee's own site: **Janssen, Cardinal,
McKesson, AmerisourceBergen, Teva, Allergan, CVS, Walgreens, Walmart, Kroger,
Purdue and the Sacklers, and the generic manufacturers.**

**Received so far [S]**, Johns Hopkins national dashboard: **~$6 billion across
2022-2023, and over $6.5 billion in 2024 alone.** Payouts run roughly two decades.

## 2. The number this finding exists for [S]

KFF Health News, with Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health and
Shatterproof — a year-long investigation, outreach to all 50 states, hundreds of
spending reports extracted line by line, **more than 7,000 catalogued uses**:

> "States and localities received more than $6 billion in opioid settlement funds in
> 2022 and 2023. According to public records, they **spent or committed about a
> third** of that amount and **set aside about another third** for future use. **The
> final third was untrackable, as many jurisdictions did not produce public reports
> on the funds.**"

**One third of two billion dollars cannot be followed, because nobody was required to
say where it went.**

**[I] This is the same defect this case file has now documented four times.**
Finding 56 in SEVENFOLD: no instrument exists to count 7-OH deaths. Finding 03: the
worst-affected age band is inside a bucket nobody disaggregates. Finding 05: the
survey counts the stock and not the flow. **Here the money itself is the thing that
went uncounted.** In every instance the data was collectable and nobody was obliged
to collect it.

## 3. And here is the part that cuts the other way [S]

**Average state allocation of settlement funds:**

| Category | Share |
|---|---|
| Addiction and mental health treatment | **18%** |
| **Recovery services — housing, transportation, legal aid** | **14%** |
| Harm reduction, including overdose reversal | 11% |
| Prevention | 9% |
| Syringe services | 2% |

**Fourteen percent to recovery support.**

**Set that against Finding 07.** Federal research money splits pharmacology against
recovery support at between **6.9 and 17.4 to one**. The settlement money puts
**18% into treatment and 14% into housing, transportation and legal aid** — close to
parity.

**[I] The states are allocating this money closer to what the evidence in Finding 02
supports than NIH is allocating its research money.** That is not the result this
case file expected to find and it is reported because it is the result. **Whoever
wrote those allocation categories understood something the research portfolio does
not reflect.**

**Two limits on that, immediately.** These are **allocation** percentages, not
outcomes — nothing here says the money worked. And they are averages across the
**two thirds that reported**. The untraceable third could be distributed any way at
all.

## 4. The $240 million that was not remediation [S]

> "Governments reported spending **more than $240 million on purposes that did not
> qualify as opioid remediation**. (Most settlements allow states to spend up to 15%
> of their funds this way.) Most of this tranche went to **legal fees**, but several
> jurisdictions **funneled money to their general fund. One county even sent funds to
> its road and bridge department.**"

And the itemized end of it:

> "$33.07 to an **anti-abortion pregnancy center** in Sandborn, Indiana, and **$30,362
> to screen first responders for heart disease** in Oregon City, Oregon."

> "from **$11.74 to buy postage** in Yavapai County, Arizona, to more than **$51
> million to increase the addiction treatment workforce** in California."

**Read the range in that last line before drawing conclusions from the first.** The
same database that caught $11.74 of postage also caught California putting $51
million into the treatment workforce. **The absurd line items are real and they are
not representative, and a case file that quotes only the postage is doing to this
data what an opponent would do to ours.**

**What is load-bearing is the $240 million and the road and bridge department** — not
because it is large against $58 billion, but because it is **what the reporting
regime permits**, and because the third that filed nothing is not in that figure at
all.

## 5. The transparency promise, checked [S]

KFF Health News with Christine Minhee, 2024-11-07. A 2023 analysis found **12 states
had committed in writing to publicly report 100% of their expenditures in a way an
average person could find and understand.** The other **38 promised less.**

The twelve: **Arizona, Colorado, Delaware, Idaho, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Missouri,
New Hampshire, New Jersey, Oregon, South Carolina, Utah.**

**Revisited as of 2024-10-09: "12 States Promised 100% Transparency on Opioid
Settlements. 7 Delivered."**

And the seven who did are not necessarily legible:

> "In fiscal year 2023, the city of Chubbuck spent about **$39,000 on Section G,
> Subsection 9**. Public Health District No. 6 spent more than **$26,000 on Section
> B, Subsection 2**. **Cracking that code requires a separate document.**"

**[I] Compliance and disclosure are not the same thing.** Idaho met the letter of its
commitment. A person who lost someone and wants to know where the money went is
handed a subsection number and a second document to look it up in. **That is the
[[Finding 05]] pattern again in a different register: the information technically
exists and reaches nobody it was meant for.**

## 6. What this finding does NOT claim

- **Every figure is [S].** KFF, Johns Hopkins and Shatterproof built the database
  from primary state filings. **OWG has not pulled those filings.** The line-item
  database is public and pulling it is the next step. **Do not cite any of these
  numbers as primary.**
- **The snapshot is old.** The database covers 2022-2023, published 2024-12-16, and
  KFF says plainly it "does not account for further spending in 2024." **Two more
  years of money have moved since.**
- **KFF says the data cannot support state comparisons:** "The differences in how
  states control, process, and report on the money make apples-to-apples comparisons
  nearly impossible." **This file makes no state rankings and neither should any OWG
  output.**
- **No claim of misuse is made against any named jurisdiction.** The examples are
  KFF's reporting of what governments themselves filed.
- **Nothing here measures whether any of it worked.**
