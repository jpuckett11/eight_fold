# EIGHTFOLD / FINDING 17
## The quantitative series exists, and it says something more interesting than the slogan. The market is not inventing new molecules faster. It is circulating more of the ones it already invented — detections doubled in four years while first-discoveries stayed flat.

**Status:** **CONFIRMED [P]** from five annual reports of a federally funded drug
early-warning system, with the one OCR-dependent year independently cross-checked.
**This finding partially contradicts the simple version of the case file's own
thesis and is written that way on purpose.**
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-31.
**Opened in pursuit of Jay Puckett's question — "stricter laws bringing more
molecules" — after Finding 16 identified CFSRE as the better instrument.**

Exhibits in `exhibits/nps_series/`.

---

## 1. The series [P]

CFSRE **NPS Discovery**, Year in Review 2021 through 2025. An open-access drug
early-warning system funded in part by the **National Institute of Justice**. Figures
read from each report's own summary paragraph.

| Year | New NPS discovered that year | Distinct NPS detected | Total detections |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | **27** | 97 | >2,700 |
| 2022 | **21** | 76 | >2,200 |
| 2023 | **17** | 79 | >3,600 |
| 2024 | **20** | 103 | >5,200 |
| 2025 | **27** | 134 | **>5,700** |

Cumulative since 2018: **201 newly discovered NPS**, **315 identified in samples**,
**more than 25,000 total detections**.

## 2. What the numbers actually say

**First discoveries per year: 27, 21, 17, 20, 27.** **Flat. Not accelerating.**

**Total detections per year: 2,700 → 5,700. Up 111%.**

**Distinct NPS in circulation per year: 97 → 134. Up 38%.**

**[I] The naive version of "stricter laws bring more molecules" predicts the first
row rises. It does not.** What rises is how much of the existing catalogue is actually
turning up in samples, and how many different compounds are in circulation at once.

**[I] The better description is not that the market invents faster under pressure. It
is that nothing ever leaves.** A compound discovered in 2019 is still being detected
in 2025, alongside a hundred and thirty-three others. **Scheduling removes a molecule
from legality. It does not remove it from the catalogue, and the catalogue only ever
grows.**

That is a **weaker** claim than "prohibition breeds new drugs" and a **harder** one to
answer, because it does not depend on any causal story about chemists reacting to
statutes. **It only requires that supply persists and variety accumulates, which the
data shows directly.**

## 3. The control problem, stated because it is fatal if ignored

**Finding 15 §5 named this in advance: a rising count of detections is partly a rising
ability to detect.**

**CFSRE's own program grew across this exact window.** The 2021 report cites NIJ award
2020-DQ-BX-0007; by 2023 the funding is a different and larger award,
15PNIJ-22-GG-04434-MUMU, for "Implementation of NPS Discovery." **More funding, more
laboratories, better instruments, more samples.**

**So the 111% rise in detections cannot be read as a 111% rise in the drug supply, and
this finding does not read it that way.**

**What survives the control:** the **flat first-discovery line**. If the rise were
purely detection capacity, novel discoveries should climb too — a better-resourced lab
finds more new things, not just more of the old ones. **It did not climb.** That
asymmetry is the finding: **capacity grew, detections grew, and the rate of genuinely
new compounds did not.**

## 4. How this squares with Finding 16, which it does not contradict

Finding 16 quoted CFSRE and the Colombo Plan directly:

> "Following China's class-wide scheduling of fentanyl compounds in May 2019, the
> nitazene class... started appearing worldwide. Likewise, following China's
> core-structure scheduling of nitazene compounds in July 2025, the orphine class...
> are now appearing worldwide. **This appears to be a recurring** [pattern]"

**Both are true and they operate at different scales.** At the level of an individual
**class**, scheduling is followed by a **substitute class** — that is Finding 16, and
CFSRE says it themselves. At the level of the **whole NPS landscape**, the rate of
novel appearance is **steady**, because the substitution is a rotation within a roughly
constant rate of innovation, not an acceleration of it.

**[I] One class dies and another takes its share. The total does not spike, because
the market was never short of options.** That is worse than an acceleration, not
better: it means the supply is not being reduced at all, only reshuffled.

## 5. Method note: the year that needed two independent reads

**The 2022 report has a thin text layer.** Its figures were obtained by **OCR**
(pdftoppm at 200 dpi, tesseract), returning "137 newly discovered" and 76 detected.

**Independently**, 2022's new-discovery count derives arithmetically from the 2023
report: 154 cumulative minus 17 discovered in 2023 minus 116 cumulative at end-2021 =
**21**, and 116 + 21 = **137**.

**The OCR and the arithmetic agree. Neither was accepted alone.** An OCR figure in a
case file that has already lost an evening to an unverified edit does not get to stand
on its own.

## 6. What this finding does NOT claim

- **It does not refute Finding 16.** §4 explains how both hold.
- **It does not measure the drug supply.** It measures what one laboratory network
  detected, with growing capacity, in the samples it received.
- **It does not attribute any change to any scheduling action.** No interrupted time
  series has been run. **Finding 15 §5 still specifies the design and it is still not
  done.**
- **"More than 2,700" and "more than 5,700" are CFSRE's own rounded phrasings.** The
  111% is computed from rounded figures and should be read as "roughly doubled."
- **NPS detections are not overdose deaths.** Nothing here counts harm.
