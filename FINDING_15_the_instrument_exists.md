## 2. The instrument he is asking for exists [P]

The question "do stricter laws bring more molecules" needs a dated series of new
molecules. **DEA publishes one.** The **National Forensic Laboratory Information
System** issues a "New Substance Public Alert" each time its laboratories identify a
compound for the first time.

**Six were linked from the NFLIS front page on the capture date. DEA's own document
IDs run DEA-SFL1-DOSE-26-002 through -006, so the 2026 series is at least six.**

| March 2026 | JWH-018-S | synthetic cannabinoid |
| March 2026 | sec-butyl butylone | cathinone |
| **May 2026** | **5-Chloro Desmethyl Chlorphine** | benzimidazolone opioid |
| **June 2026** | **N-Propionitrile 5-Chloro Desmethylbrorphine** | benzimidazolone opioid |

## 5. Puckett's estimation proposal, assessed honestly

> *"couldnt we estimate by the total people with addiction in each age bracket
> correlating to stricter laws bringing more molecules"*

**The instinct is right and the design has a problem he himself identified earlier
tonight.**

**What works:**
- **The outcome variable is real and dated.** NFLIS first-identification alerts give a
  molecule count with months attached.
- **The exposure variable is real and dated.** Scheduling actions are Federal Register
  documents with dates. **EIGHTFOLD's own DEA scheduling watcher has 139 baselined.**

**What does not work: the age brackets.**

**This is the objection Puckett made to Aegis three hours ago and it applies to his
own proposal now.** From `METHOD.md`, written at his instruction:

> *"a mortgage borrower at 18-29 has a bad divorce at 30 and spirals. Breaking this
> into an age range does and proves nothing."*

**SUD prevalence by age bracket cannot attribute a molecule's appearance to a law.**
The bands hold different people each year, prevalence moves for a dozen reasons at
once, and no individual in any band is linked to any scheduling action. **It is an
ecological design and it would produce a correlation that a competent opponent would
dismantle in a paragraph.**

**The design that answers the question needs no age brackets at all:**

> **An interrupted time series.** Scheduling actions as dated interventions; NFLIS
> first-identifications as the dated outcome; the unit of analysis is the **molecule**,
> not the person. Test whether the rate of novel identifications rises after
> class-wide scheduling actions, and whether the **structural distance** of each new
> compound from the scheduled one shrinks.

**And it needs a control, or it is worthless.** NFLIS reporting volume rises over time
because laboratories, submissions and instruments improve. **A rising count of novel
identifications is partly a rising ability to identify.** The control is
identifications of **long-scheduled** substances over the same period: if novel
identifications rise while established ones stay flat, the rise is real. If both rise
together, it is detection.

**What is missing to run it:** a complete dated alert series. **The six captured are
what the NFLIS front page happened to link on one day.** The full historical list
renders client-side on `publicationsRedesign.xhtml` and did not come back in the
served HTML. **That is the pull that unlocks this analysis, and it needs a browser.**

## 6. What this finding does NOT claim

- **No correlation is computed and none is asserted.** §5 is a design, not a result.
- **Six alerts is not a rate.** They are one day's front-page links, not a census.
- **NFLIS counts laboratory identifications, not use, not harm and not deaths.** A
  molecule appearing in one seizure is not a market.
- **The CDC quotations are [P] in content but the PDF is uncaptured** because cdc.gov
  403s scripts.
- **Nothing here says scheduling caused these compounds.** The sequence is suggestive
  and §5 exists because suggestive is not enough.
