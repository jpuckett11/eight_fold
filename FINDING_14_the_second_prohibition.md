# EIGHTFOLD / FINDING 14
## Enforcement worked. Prescribing fell. And the patients who were on those prescriptions went to the emergency room at 1.68 times the rate for overdose and 2.28 times the rate for mental health crisis. The crackdown produced its own casualties and they are not counted anywhere in it.

**Status:** **CONFIRMED [P]** from three cohort studies totalling over 860,000
patients — **and §4 carries a 2026 study that cuts against the causal reading, because
it exists.**
**Analyst:** Aegis, for Obsidian Watch Group. **Date:** 2026-08-31.
**Opened at Jay Puckett's reading of Finding 13:**

> *"right but what does this do? again more prohibition in the form of medicine, real
> pain patients then forced to find another method for help"*

**That is the correct question to ask of Finding 13, and it has an answer with
numbers on it.**

Exhibits in `exhibits/tapering/`. **Direct continuation of SEVENFOLD Finding 55.**

---

## 1. The structure he is describing

Finding 13 documents a real failure: 90,000 refusal-to-fill forms, a policy that
forbade pharmacists to cut off a pill mill, a compliance lead who called overdose kits
"More BS." **The enforcement response to that failure was to make dispensing riskier
for pharmacies and prescribing riskier for doctors.**

**[I] Puckett's point is that this does not distinguish between the pill mill and the
patient.** A pharmacy that fears liability declines the ambiguous prescription. A
physician tapers the stable patient. **The enforcement instrument acts on the supply,
and the supply is shared between the people it is aimed at and the people it is not.**

## 2. What happened to the patients, measured [P]

**Agnoli et al., *JAMA* 2021, PMID 34342618.** OptumLabs claims, 2008-2019.
**113,618 patients across 203,920 stable baseline periods**, all on ≥50 morphine
milligram equivalents per day for a full 12 months before anything changed. Tapering
defined as ≥15% relative dose reduction.

| Outcome | Tapered | Not tapered | Adjusted IRR |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overdose events / 100 person-years** | **9.3** | 5.5 | **1.68** (1.53-1.85) |
| **Mental health crisis / 100 person-years** | **7.6** | 3.3 | **2.28** (1.96-2.65) |

**And the speed matters.** Each 10% increase in maximum monthly dose-reduction
velocity was associated with **aIRR 1.09 for overdose and 1.18 for mental health
crisis.** **The faster the taper, the worse the outcome, in a dose-response.**

**It is not short-lived.** *JAMA Network Open* 2022, PMID 35696163, 21,515 tapering
events in 19,377 patients, exposure-crossover design: **aIRR 1.57 for overdose or
withdrawal and 1.52 for mental health crisis**, with the authors concluding the
elevated rates extend **up to two years after taper initiation**.

**These are people who had been stable for a year on a prescribed dose.**

## 3. It was not a fringe concern at the time [P]

*Substance Abuse* 2019, PMID 31361588. On **2019-03-06**, a public letter went to CDC
about "**the widespread misapplication of its 2016 guideline**." Signed by **318
health care professionals and three former Directors of the White House Office of
National Drug Control Policy.**

The letter concerned "**initiatives and policies by payers, quality metric agencies,
health care organizations, and other regulators enforced to strongly incentivize or
mandate forced opioid dose reductions on long-term opioid recipients who were
otherwise stable.**"

**Both FDA and the CDC Director issued corrective statements in April 2019.** The
guideline itself was revised in 2022.

**[I] The mechanism Puckett names is not a hypothesis. It was identified by the
field, in writing, with three former drug czars signing, and the agency conceded it.**

## 4. The study that cuts against this, reported because it exists [P]

***Journal of General Internal Medicine*, 2026, PMID 42343056.** The largest of them:
**732,038 adults**, within-individual design, Optum Clinformatics 2010-2021.

> "This within-individual study found that LTOT recipients who experienced a dose
> taper had greater risk of SUD or overdose around the onset of the taper. **Much of
> that risk escalation may precede—rather than result from—changes in monthly dose.**"

**Read that carefully. It is the strongest challenge to the causal story and it is the
newest and largest study in the set.** If risk is already climbing *before* the taper,
then some of what looks like tapering harm is **doctors tapering patients who were
already deteriorating** — the taper as a marker of trouble rather than its cause.

**This finding does not resolve that**, and the JAMA authors flag the same limit
themselves: *"interpretation is limited by the observational study design."*

**[I] What survives the challenge:** the **velocity** result. A faster taper being
worse than a slower one, in a graded relationship, is hard to explain by
reverse causation — the physician's *rate* of reduction is not plausibly set by the
patient's future overdose. **And the 2026 study says "much," not "all."**

## 5. Where this connects, and it is the spine of both case files

**SEVENFOLD Finding 55** established the precedent: OxyContin was reformulated in
2010 to be abuse-deterrent, and heroin deaths went from **3,036 to 15,469**. Powell
and Pacula, NBER 26988, established it as a substitution effect rather than a
coincidence.

**SEVENFOLD Finding 56** established that the government cannot count the harm of the
compound it schedules, because it never built the instrument.

**Finding 13** shows the enforcement case that produced this round of tightening.

**This finding is what happens next, and it has happened before.**

> **[I] The pattern is not that prohibition fails. It is that prohibition succeeds at
> exactly the thing it measures — prescriptions written, pills dispensed, molecules
> scheduled — and the people who were relying on the thing it removed are not in any
> of those measures.**

Puckett's phrase for it, from Finding 20 of SEVENFOLD: *"they will just keep going
after a molecule, the market will remain."* **This finding is the same sentence
pointed at medicine instead of the street.**

## 6. And the number nobody has

**There is no count of people who lost legitimate pain treatment and died.**

Not because it is hidden. **Because the same instrument gap Finding 56 documented
applies here.** Overdose death certificates record the substance found. They do not
record what the decedent was prescribed the year before, whether it was tapered, or
who tapered it. **The Agnoli cohort exists only because a commercial claims warehouse
happened to link pharmacy and medical records for insured patients.**

**[I] So the harms of the crackdown are visible only in insured populations with
continuous coverage — which is to say, the least vulnerable people it affected.**
The uninsured, the Medicaid-churned and the incarcerated are outside every one of
these cohorts.

## 7. What this finding does NOT claim

- **It does not say the enforcement was wrong.** Finding 13 documents 90,000 ignored
  warnings. **Both things are true and this file holds both.**
- **It does not claim tapering causes overdose.** §4 is in the finding precisely
  because the largest and newest study says much of the risk may precede the taper.
  **The association is established; the causation is contested.**
- **It does not claim a number for people harmed.** §6 says no such number exists.
- **It does not oppose tapering.** The literature supports *slow, consensual*
  tapering; the velocity finding argues against *forced and fast*, not against the
  practice.
- **All three cohorts are observational and insured-population only.**
