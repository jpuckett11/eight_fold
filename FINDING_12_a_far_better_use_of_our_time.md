## 1. What DOJ says happened [P]

Justice Department and DEA, **$50 million settlement with Walmart Inc.** to resolve
allegations that its pharmacies "illegally filled thousands of invalid prescriptions
for opioids and other controlled substances" in violation of the Controlled
Substances Act.

**The complaint was filed 2020-12-22, amended in 2022, and covers conduct since
2013-06-26.** Thirteen years of conduct. Six years of litigation.

## 2. The part that is not about money [P]

> "The United States alleged that members of Walmart's compliance team **knew that
> certain prescribers were operating as 'pill mills' but filled invalid prescriptions
> written by those prescribers anyway.** Members of the compliance team allegedly knew
> of the prescribers' egregious conduct **because Walmart's own pharmacists reported
> the conduct to Walmart's corporate compliance team, including through thousands of
> 'refusal-to-fill' forms.**"

**The pharmacists saw it. The pharmacists wrote it down. Thousands of times. It went
up the chain and stopped.**

And then the sentence this finding is named for:

> "The compliance team, however, prioritized other goals over CSA compliance. **As one
> director on the compliance team acknowledged in an email, rather than analyzing the
> refusal-to-fill reports, the compliance team viewed '[d]riving sales and patient
> awareness,' as 'a far better use of our Market Directors and Market manger's
> time.'**"

**A far better use of our time.**

DOJ also alleges the pharmacists themselves filled prescriptions they knew were
invalid, bearing "obvious red flags such as dangerous combinations of opioids,
'cocktails' of opioids and non-opioids, excessively repeated fills of high-dosages of
often-abused opioids, or repeated requests for early fills."

**[I] This is the counting problem from SEVENFOLD Finding 56 inverted.** That finding
established the government has no instrument to count 7-OH deaths. **Here the
instrument existed, worked, and produced thousands of records — and the organization
holding them decided reading them was a worse use of staff time than selling.**

## 3. What it cost, and what it did not settle [P]

**$50 million**, plus a memorandum of agreement with DEA requiring Walmart to build a
reporting hotline, monitor its own dispensing patterns, and evaluate suspect
prescribers. **Those are controls a reader might reasonably have assumed already
existed.**

And DOJ's own final line, quoted in full because it must travel with everything above:

> "**The claims resolved by the settlement are allegations only; there has been no
> determination of liability.**"

**Nothing in this finding asserts Walmart's guilt.** What is established is what the
United States alleged and what Walmart agreed to pay.

## 4. Puckett's second claim, tested [P arithmetic on [S] inputs]

> *"that alone could fund this"*

**SAMHSA's entire grant program is $19.00 billion a year** — computed from the 3,000
FY2024-25 awards captured in Finding 09, $37.99B over two years.

**One year of federal improper payments is $186 billion** (GAO-26-108694, [S]).

| One year of improper payments equals | |
|---|---|
| **Years of SAMHSA's entire grant program** | **9.8** |
| Multiples of the whole 20-year opioid settlement | 3.18 |
| Per untreated adult with a substance use disorder (40.7M) | **$4,570** |
| **Per adult who actually perceives an unmet need (1.8M)** | **$103,333** |
| Times the 24 SAMHSA employment-titled grants | **2,520x** |
| Times Indiana's entire local employment spending | **1,743,434x** |

**He is right, and the sharpest form of it is the last row but one.** Finding 05
established that of 40.7 million untreated adults with a substance use disorder,
**only 1.8 million perceive an unmet need** — the people actively asking. **One year of
what the federal government cannot account for is $103,333 for every one of them.**

**Now the honest counterweights, because this number is powerful enough to be worth
protecting from overstatement:**

- **Improper payments are not a pot of money.** They are already-appropriated funds
  paid to the wrong party, in the wrong amount, or without adequate documentation.
  **Recovering them does not free a budget line; it returns money to programs that
  already have claims on it.** "This could fund X" is a comparison of scale, not a
  funding proposal, and this file states it as scale.
- **The $186B is [S].** `gao.gov` returns HTTP 403 to scripted fetch and
  `paymentaccuracy.gov` serves an identical shell for every path. **Capturing the
  primary remains open.**
- **$103,333 per person assumes a transfer that no mechanism exists to make.**

## 5. "Because of a simple mistake" — the half to be careful with [S]

**GAO reports about 82%, roughly $153 billion, are overpayments**, and the driver
across the largest programs is documentation and eligibility verification rather than
fraud. **So the description is broadly right: much of it is process failure, not
theft.**

**But "simple" is doing work that should be checked.** GAO also found that of the 24
agencies responsible for most improper payments, **only half fully complied with the
Payment Integrity Information Act** — the statute that requires them to measure this
properly. **19 programs ran improper payment rates of at least 10%; six exceeded
25%.**

**[I] A single clerical error is a simple mistake. A quarter of a program's payments
going out improperly, for years, while half the responsible agencies fail to comply
with the law requiring them to measure it, is not a mistake. It is a system that does
not treat the number as a problem.** That distinction matters, because "simple
mistake" invites the reply that mistakes are unavoidable — and a 25% error rate is not
a mistake anyone is trying to avoid.

## 6. The two halves of this finding are the same fact

**[I] Walmart's pharmacists produced thousands of reports and a compliance director
decided reading them was a worse use of time than driving sales. Federal agencies
produce a $186 billion improper-payment figure every year and half of the largest
ones cannot comply with the law requiring them to measure it properly.**

**In both cases the information existed and nobody's job depended on acting on it.**

That is the pattern this case file has now recorded six times: SEVENFOLD Finding 56,
and EIGHTFOLD Findings 03, 05, 08, 09 and now 12. **Every time, the data was
collectable. Every time, no one was obliged to collect or read it. And every time,
the people on the other end of the failure were people with addictions.**

## 7. What this finding does NOT claim

- **No determination of liability against Walmart**, per DOJ's own language, quoted
  in §3.
- **The email is quoted as DOJ quoted it.** The underlying document has not been
  obtained and its full context is unknown.
- **The $186B and its composition are [S]**, from GAO's product page, primary not
  captured.
- **No claim that improper-payment money is available to redirect.** §4 says the
  opposite explicitly.
- **No claim about Walmart's current practices.** The memorandum of agreement is
  forward-looking and this file does not assess it.
